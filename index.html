About Me

From a county desk to high availability.

Fifteen years ago I was an administrative assistant for Jefferson County, Missouri. Today I design the high-availability infrastructure behind clinical communication systems. The path between those two things ran through a help desk, a channel support role, and a lot of nights taking things apart to see how they worked.

I joined Ricoh in 2015 as a support analyst and spent nine years there. I moved through channel account management, which taught me discovery, requirements gathering, and how to explain a complex system to someone who doesn't live in it, and then into technology design, architecting enterprise software solutions for large clients. Those years taught me that the hard part of infrastructure is rarely the technology. It's understanding what the people using it actually need it to do.

In June 2024 I moved to Primary Systems to go fully hands-on. I came in as an IT Systems Engineer I, moved to II in 2025, and to III in August 2026. The work is high-availability infrastructure for clinical environments: virtualization, SQL Server Always On availability groups, identity, integrations, and the monitoring that keeps all of it honest. In healthcare the stakes stay legible. When a system goes quiet, a nurse call doesn't route.

My home lab is where I test ideas before they go anywhere that matters, build tools I actually use, and pick up skills the day job doesn't hand me. I document everything as I go, because a project I can't explain is a project I only half understand.

Outside of work: two kids, a home lab that is always one project deeper than planned, and an unreasonable amount of emotional investment in Mizzou and the Cardinals.

Architecture

Decisions I owned

The reasoning matters more than the deployment. Client and site details are deliberately generic.

Monitoring the dependency, not the host

We were monitoring patient activity. The trouble with watching the output is that it can't distinguish a quiet ward from a broken pipeline — both look like no data.

Nurse call data arrives raw and has to be translated into the activity table, and SQL Server Service Broker performs that translation. When Service Broker fails, the raw data keeps arriving and the activity table simply stops filling. Every host still answers its ping. The activity view just looks like a slow night.

So I moved monitoring off the output and onto the mechanism: PRTG sensors against Service Broker itself and against availability group health, so an alert fires when translation stops rather than when someone eventually notices the numbers look wrong. Monitoring the result tells you something is wrong, late. Monitoring the dependency tells you what broke, now.

PRTGSQL Service Broker Availability MonitoringFailure-mode design

Retiring a tier that wasn't earning its place

One inherited environment ran three database roles across three SQL Servers, with an asynchronous replica standing by for disaster recovery. It worked. But the DR tier carried real cost and ongoing complexity for a recovery scenario that environment didn't actually need.

The architecture I designed instead pairs two SQL Servers for the transactional tier and two for the warehouse tier, and drops the asynchronous DR portion entirely.

Removing infrastructure is a harder call than adding it. You have to be right about what the system genuinely requires, and you own the outcome if you're wrong. Standardizing on one shape across deployments is also what makes an environment supportable by someone who wasn't there when it was built — most of the difference between an installation and an architecture.

Stepping a host fleet off end-of-support

I was responsible for upgrading our hosts off VMware ESXi 5.5. There's no direct path from 5.5 to current, so it ran as a stepped upgrade — 5.5 to 6, then 6.5, then 7 on a subset of hosts — migrating VMs between hosts as capacity freed up.

It ran inside an eight-hour downtime window, which in an environment supporting clinical operations is the constraint every other decision bends around. Most of that window went on problems that appear in no upgrade guide: getting Dell OpenManage onto the hosts, and fighting VIB installation scripts.

What I took from it is that the upgrade path is the easy part to plan. The vendor tooling around it is what actually consumes the window, and that's what the estimate needs to be built on.

VMware ESXiStaged Upgrade VM MigrationDell OpenManage Lifecycle Management

Re-IPing a clinical stack into a new subnet

When the hosts moved, they moved to an entirely new subnet. I was responsible for re-IPing everything that lived on the old one: the failover clusters, the availability group environment, the full clinical communications stack, and the NetScaler load balancer sitting in front of it.

This is unforgiving work because the pieces reference each other. Listener IPs, cluster resources, DNS, and the load balancer's backend definitions all have to move together, and a stack that comes back in a partly-migrated state can report healthy while failover is quietly broken — the worst version of the failure, because nobody finds out until the day it's needed.

Everything came back online.
